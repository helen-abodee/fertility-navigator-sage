2 · Gaps and the two conflicts

Conflict C1: insurance coverage

D2 (2017) says preservation procedures are "usually not covered" by insurance. D4 (2025) says California commercial fully-insured plans are mandated to cover them under SB 600 (2019). D2 simply predates the law. Proposed: use D4 for all insurance content, never cite D2's financial section.

Conflict C2: dead organization

D2 points patients to Fertile Hope and its Sharing Hope program as a live organization. Fertile Hope was absorbed into Livestrong years ago; D4 lists only Livestrong Fertility. Proposed: use D4's financial aid table, never D2's resource list.

The gaps that matter most

  • No document maps situations to options. Which options fit a 24-year-old with ovaries who hasn't started chemo, versus someone mid-treatment, is the core of the tool and is not in these PDFs. Likely source: the ASCO 2025 guideline that D3 and D4 both cite. I need either that document or your sign-off to pull it as a source.
  • No procedure walkthroughs. The egg-freezing timeline (10 to 14 days of shots, retrieval under sedation) and the sperm, embryo, and ovarian tissue equivalents appear nowhere. Needs a patient-education source.
  • Ovarian suppression during treatment is in your spec and in zero documents. It stays hidden until sourced.
  • The 2026 California IVF law is not in any document. And D4 states Medi-Cal did NOT cover fertility preservation as of June 1, 2025, which shapes the Medi-Cal branch until we verify what the new law changed.
  • Survivor labs (AMH, FSH, LH) and the 100-days-after-chemo timing: not in the documents. Expected in Hannah's risk document.
  • Partner-clinic labels need your written list. The source docs explicitly say their clinic lists are not endorsements, so the "partner" designation has to come from you, not from them.

The full list (15 gaps) is in content/GAPS.md.

3 · The question flow

Ten questions, one per screen. The treatment-stage question moved up to Q2 because it forks everything after it: survivors get a different results spine (testing and current options, no pre-treatment urgency), and nobody gets asked a question that doesn't apply to them. "Not sure" always routes to real content, never a dead end.

  1. Welcome
    What this tool is, what it never asks, about 3 minutes
  2. Q1
    Who is this for?
    Just me / me and my partner. "Just me" listed first; most users are single.
  3. Q2
    Where are you with treatment?
    The fork. Haven't started / in treatment / finished. "Finished" asks how long ago and switches the results page to the survivor spine.
  4. Q3
    How old are you?
  5. Q4
    Which describes your body?
    Asked as anatomy (ovaries and/or uterus, testes), with self-describe and prefer-not-to-say. One line on why it matters. Draft wording is open question 1 for you.
  6. Q5
    What type of cancer? optional
    Only feeds the questions-for-your-doctor list. Open question: keep in v1?
  7. Q6
    What treatment is planned or happened?
    Multi-select: chemo, radiation (with body region), surgery, transplant, not sure. Chemo drug names optional, held for the risk module.
  8. Q7
    How do you feel about having children someday?
    Yes / maybe / not sure / no. "No" still ends with a short, pressure-free results page.
  9. Q8
    What health insurance do you have?
    CA job/private plan, Medi-Cal, other, none, don't know. "Don't know" routes to the D4 benefit-verification guide. Never asks for IDs or plan names.
  10. Q9
    Does your partner have insurance through work? only if partnered
  11. Q10
    ZIP code optional
    "Used only to measure distance to clinics on this device. It never leaves your phone."
  12. Results
    Options that fit, what each involves, costs with sources, insurance path, financial aid, questions for your doctor, clinic map, PDF download

4 · Design direction

Warm paper with a green-gray cast, ink that leans green instead of black, and a single deep fir accent. Calm and alive without being a wellness brand, and nowhere near hospital blue. Headings in Literata, a warm book serif. Body text in Atkinson Hyperlegible, which the Braille Institute designed for low-vision readers; for an audience that includes people reading through chemo fatigue on a phone, the accessible face is also the right aesthetic choice.
