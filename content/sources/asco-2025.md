## 1. Fertility preservation methods

### Males

- **Sperm cryopreservation (sperm banking) — recommended, Strong / High quality evidence.** "Cryopreservation of ejaculated sperm (sperm banking) should be offered prior to initiating cancer-directed therapy. Health care clinicians should discuss sperm banking with all pubertal and postpubertal males prior to receiving cancer treatment." Qualifying statement: fertility clinicians "empirically recommend a minimum of three ejaculates of sufficient quality," but clinicians should be flexible and "collect as many ejaculates as possible before the start of gonadotoxic therapy"; "any cryopreserved sperm can offer a chance for biological parenthood."
  - Applies to: pubertal and postpubertal males. Timing: before cancer-directed therapy.
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 3.1 + qualifying statement.

- **Testicular sperm extraction (TESE) — recommended, Strong / High quality evidence.** "TESE with sperm cryopreservation should be offered to pubertal and postpubertal males who cannot produce a semen sample, before cancer treatment begins."
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 3.2.

- **Hormonal gonadoprotection in males — recommended AGAINST, Strong / High quality evidence.** "Hormonal suppression therapy should not be offered to males as an approach for preserving fertility. It is not effective and therefore not recommended."
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 3.3.

- **Testicular tissue cryopreservation — investigational, Strong / Very low quality evidence.** "Other methods, such as testicular tissue cryopreservation in pre-pubertal males and reimplantation or grafting of human testicular tissue, should be performed only as part of clinical trials or approved experimental protocols."
  - Applies to: prepubertal males. Status: investigational / clinical trial only (also called "currently investigational" in Recommendation 5.1).
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 3.4; see also 5.1.

- **Post-treatment sperm collection (males) — Strong / Low quality evidence.** "Males should be advised of a potentially higher risk of genetic damage in sperm collected soon after initiation and completion of antineoplastic and/or radiation therapy. It is strongly recommended that sperm be collected before initiation of treatment because the quality of the sample and sperm DNA integrity may be compromised after single treatment." Time pressure or already-diminished counts "should not dissuade patients from banking sperm"; intracytoplasmic sperm injection "allows the future use of a very limited amount of sperm."
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 3.5.

### Females

- **Embryo cryopreservation — established, Strong / High quality evidence.** "Embryo cryopreservation should be offered as it is an established fertility preservation method, and it has routinely been used for storing embryos after in vitro fertilization."
  - Timing: before gonadotoxic therapy (see qualifying statement below); also available post-treatment (Rec 4.3).
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 4.1.

- **Mature oocyte cryopreservation — established, Strong / High quality evidence.** "Cryopreservation of unfertilized oocytes should be offered as it is an established fertility preservation method and may be especially well suited to females who do not have a male partner, do not wish to use donor sperm, or have religious or ethical objections to embryo freezing. Oocyte cryopreservation should be performed in centers with the necessary expertise."
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 4.2.

- **Qualifying statement for 4.1/4.2 (timing and stimulation):** both are "recommended options for fertility preservation in female patients with cancer undergoing gonadotoxic therapy"; shared decision-making among oncology team, reproductive endocrinology team, and patient is emphasized. "Timing of this procedure no longer depends on the menstrual cycle in most cases ... oocyte harvesting for the purpose of oocyte or embryo cryopreservation is now possible on a cycle day–independent schedule." For estrogen-sensitive breast and gynecologic malignancies, "Aromatase inhibitor–based stimulation protocols are now well established ... there is no increased cancer recurrence risk as a result of aromatase inhibitor–supplemented ovarian stimulation."
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Qualifying Statements for Recommendations 4.1 and 4.2.

- **In vitro maturation (IVM) of oocytes — emerging method, Conditional / Low quality evidence.** "IVM of oocytes may be offered as an emerging FP method." Qualifying statement: "IVM has lower pregnancy and live birth rates compared to IVF in females without cancer. The pregnancy and live birth rates of IVM in cancer survivors is unknown."
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 4.4 + qualifying statement.

- **Ovarian transposition (oophoropexy) — recommended option, Strong / Moderate quality evidence.** "Ovarian transposition (oophoropexy) may be offered to reproductive-aged patients when pelvic irradiation is required. However, because of radiation scatter, ovaries are not always protected, and patients should be aware that this technique is not always successful. Because of the risk of remigration of the ovaries, this procedure should be performed as close to the time of radiation treatment as possible." Qualifying statement: "not suitable for patients with a moderate or high risk of ovarian metastasis, or those receiving concomitant gonadotoxic chemotherapy."
  - Applies to: reproductive-aged patients requiring pelvic irradiation. Timing: as close to radiation treatment as possible.
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 4.5 + qualifying statement.

- **Uterine transposition — experimental, Conditional / Low quality evidence.** "Uterine transposition in reproductive-aged patients remains experimental and should be offered only as part of a clinical trial or approved experimental protocols."
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 4.6.

- **Conservative gynecologic surgery — Strong / Moderate quality evidence.** (a) radical trachelectomy may be offered for stage IA2–IB1 cervical cancer "if the tumor diameter is <2 cm and invasion depth is < 10mm"; (b) fertility-sparing surgery may be offered for well-differentiated (grade 1) endometrial tumors with minimal myometrial invasion confirmed by MRI, with progestins (oral or IUD) as "the primary fertility-preserving option for early-stage endometrial cancer"; (c) fertility-sparing surgery may be offered for stage IA grade 1 epithelial ovarian cancer after thorough staging; uterine preservation may be considered in other stages/grades; (d) less radical surgeries may be offered in other gynecologic malignancies when clinically appropriate.
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 4.7.

- **GnRH agonists / ovarian suppression — NOT a substitute for established methods; adjunct only, Conditional / Moderate quality evidence.** ASCO's exact stance (Recommendation 4.8): "Gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonists (GnRHa) should not be used in place of established fertility preservation methods such as oocyte, embryo, or ovarian tissue cryopreservation. GnRHa may be offered as an adjunct to females with breast cancer. Beyond breast cancer, the potential benefits and risks of GnRHa warrant further investigation, and trials are encouraged." (Conditional recommendation, Moderate quality evidence.)
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 4.8.
- **GnRHa in oncologic emergencies — Conditional / Low quality evidence.** "For patients with oncologic emergencies requiring urgent chemotherapy, GnRHa may be offered and can provide benefits such as menstrual suppression." (Recommendation 4.9)
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 4.9.

- **Ovarian tissue cryopreservation (OTC) and transplantation — established, Strong / Moderate quality evidence.** "Ovarian tissue cryopreservation (OTC) for the purpose of future transplantation may be offered to patients with cancer as an established fertility preservation method. As it does not require ovarian stimulation, it can be performed immediately in those unable to delay chemotherapy. In addition, it does not require sexual maturity and hence may be the only method available in prepubertal patients. This method may also be offered as an emerging method to restore global ovarian function." It may be an alternative to, or an adjunct to, embryo/oocyte cryopreservation. Qualifying statement: "Evaluating cancer survivors for residual neoplastic cells before ovarian tissue transplantation is essential ... There is a theoretical risk of reintroducing malignant cells but the clinical significance of this is unknown. To reduce this risk, OTC may be deferred until posttreatment MRD negativity is achieved."
  - Applies to: including prepubertal patients (no sexual maturity required); can be done without delaying chemotherapy.
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 4.10 + qualifying statement.

### Children and adolescents

- **Recommendation 5.1 — Strong / Moderate quality evidence.** "Clinicians should offer established methods of fertility preservation (eg, semen or oocyte cryopreservation) in children and adolescents who have initiated puberty, with patient assent and parent or guardian consent. For prepubertal children, the only fertility preservation options are ovarian and testicular cryopreservation, the latter of which is currently investigational."
  - Note: per this wording, ovarian tissue cryopreservation is an option for prepubertal children; testicular tissue cryopreservation is explicitly "currently investigational."
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 5.1.

## 2. Discussions and referral

- "Clinicians caring for adult and pediatric patients with cancer should discuss the possibility of infertility as early as possible before treatment starts to preserve the full range of options." (Strong / Moderate)
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 1.1.
- "Clinicians should refer patients who express an interest in fertility preservation, and those who are uncertain, to reproductive specialists." (Strong / Very low)
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 1.2.
- Discussing infertility "can ultimately reduce distress and improve quality of life, even if the patient does not undergo fertility preservation." (Strong / Moderate)
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 1.3.
- Qualifying statement for 1.3/1.4: discussions must take place "with all patients, irrespective of their reproductive risk profile, current family size, cancer prognosis, sexual orientation or identity, religious beliefs, financial or insurance resources, access to care, or other potential considerations, including disparities."
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Qualifying Statement for Recommendations 1.3 and 1.4.
- "All clinicians should be prepared to discuss infertility as a potential risk of therapy. This discussion should take place as soon as possible once a cancer diagnosis is made and can occur simultaneously with staging and the formulation of a treatment plan." (Strong / Very low)
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 6.1.
- Good practice statements: refer patients interested in fertility "as well as those who are ambivalent or uncertain ... as soon as possible" (GPS 6.3); oncology teams should ensure prompt access to a multidisciplinary FP team including fertility specialists, mental-health professionals, social workers, financial counseling/insurance navigation, and genetic counselors (GPS 6.4); clinicians should advocate for comprehensive insurance coverage of FP services and long-term storage (GPS 6.5); encourage participation in registries and clinical studies (GPS 6.2).
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Good Practice Statements 6.2–6.5.

## 3. Post-treatment / survivor fertility assessment

- Ongoing survivorship discussions: "Additional discussions and/or referrals may be offered yearly when the patient returns for follow-up after completion of cancer-directed therapy or when treatment plans change or evolve, as well as if pregnancy is being considered. The discussions should be ongoing throughout survivorship and documented in the medical record." (Strong / Low)
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 1.4.
- Post-treatment embryo/oocyte cryopreservation: may be offered to patients "at risk of primary ovarian insufficiency or infertility" who did not preserve before treatment, to survivors with insufficient cryopreserved material for desired family size, and to those delaying childbearing who "face the risk of age-related fertility decline, which may be accelerated in cancer survivors." (Strong / Moderate)
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 4.3.
- Ovarian reserve testing: in the post-treatment setting, viability "can be assessed through markers such as anti-Mullerian hormone (AMH) levels and antral follicle count (AFC)."
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Qualifying Statement for Recommendation 4.3; also confirmed in the Guideline Central summary (URL 3).
- Timing after chemotherapy: "Due to timelines of oocyte development, there may be no oocyte yield within 3 months of last chemotherapy dose. Patients should be counseled on the unknown reproductive potential and offspring health of gametes obtained proximal to gonadotoxic therapy."
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Qualifying Statement for Recommendation 4.3.
- FSH, LH, estradiol, inhibin B, semen-analysis schedules, or any defined post-treatment waiting period before attempting conception: **not addressed in the sources read** (Guideline Central summary, URL 3, likewise states the guideline summary "does not address FSH, LH, or specific waiting periods beyond the 3-month mention"). The unread JCO full text may contain more.
- Individualization: Recommendation 2.1 requires risk evaluation to "consider specific patient groups known to be at increased risk" and account for "the effect of chronologic age ... for females due to increased infertility risk with concomitant aging" — i.e., assessment is framed as individualized, but no explicit survivor-testing algorithm appears in the sources read.
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 2.1.

## 4. Risk stratification by treatment

- Recommendation 2.1 (Strong / Moderate): clinicians should offer evaluation and counseling on reproductive risk, considering "specific patient groups known to be at increased risk due to the gonadotoxic nature of the therapies they receive or could receive in the future," those "on longer-term treatments that delay or preclude the ability to conceive," and "those for whom the risk remains uncertain due to the unknown reproductive toxicity of many cancer-directed therapies."
  - Cite: COG PDF (URL 1), Recommendation 2.1.
- Named risk categories (e.g., high/intermediate/low risk), alkylating-agent classes, cyclophosphamide equivalent dose (CED) thresholds, or radiation dose thresholds: **not addressed in the sources read.** The Guideline Central summary (URL 3) states the guideline summary "does not provide detailed stratification by specific treatment categories (alkylating agents, CED, radiation doses)." Any such tables would be in the unread JCO full text or its supplements.
