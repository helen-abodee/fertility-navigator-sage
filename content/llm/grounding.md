## Preservation options

### Egg freezing (established)
Mature eggs are collected from your ovaries and frozen, unfertilized, for possible use in the future.
Who it applies to: anatomy ovaries_uterus; stage not_started/finished.
- Consult and baseline tests: You meet a fertility doctor (a reproductive endocrinologist). They do a transvaginal ultrasound to look at your ovaries and count growing follicles. Blood tests check hormones related to fertility, including FSH and AMH.
- Hormone injections: You give yourself hormone shots each day to help a group of eggs mature. During this time you visit the clinic almost every other day for blood tests and ultrasounds.
- Egg retrieval: You get medicine so you sleep through it. The doctor uses an ultrasound wand and a very thin needle, passed through the wall of the vagina, to collect the mature eggs. Most people go home within an hour.
- Freezing and storage: The mature eggs are frozen and stored. They can stay frozen for as long as you want. Storage is billed every year.
Timeline: The medical part, shots to retrieval, takes about 2 weeks once it starts, and no longer has to wait for a menstrual cycle. What published numbers do not count: getting the first appointment, insurance authorization, and clinic scheduling, which vary a lot. Ask the clinic for their real start-to-finish time for cancer patients.
What to expect: Freezing eggs rather than embryos may fit especially well if you do not have a partner, do not want to use donor sperm, or prefer not to create embryos. Not every egg collected will be mature enough to freeze, and frozen eggs do not guarantee a pregnancy later. Results vary from person to person. Storage is billed every year, and there are added costs later if you thaw and use the eggs.
Cost, One egg freezing cycle: $12,400 on average (US) (Does not include medicines.) [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31)]
Cost, Fertility medicines: about $5,000 [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31)]
Cost, Storage: $200 to $500 per year [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31)]
Cost, Thawing and using your eggs later: not published (There are added costs when you are ready to thaw and use the eggs to attempt pregnancy.) [Memorial Sloan Kettering: Fertility Preservation Before Cancer Treatment (ovaries and uterus) (2019-10-23)]

### Embryo freezing (established)
Eggs are collected from your ovaries, fertilized with sperm in the lab to create embryos, and the embryos are frozen for possible use in the future.
Who it applies to: anatomy ovaries_uterus; stage not_started/finished.
- Consult and baseline tests: You meet a fertility doctor. They do a transvaginal ultrasound and blood tests for hormones related to fertility, including FSH and AMH.
- Hormone injections: You give yourself hormone shots each day to help a group of eggs mature, with clinic visits almost every other day for blood tests and ultrasounds.
- Egg retrieval: You get medicine so you sleep through it. The doctor collects the mature eggs with a thin needle guided by ultrasound. Most people go home within an hour.
- Fertilization and freezing: In the lab, the eggs are fertilized with sperm from your partner or a donor to create embryos (this is in vitro fertilization, or IVF). The embryos are frozen and stored. Storage is billed every year.
Timeline: The medical part, shots through fertilization and freezing, takes about 2 to 3 weeks once it starts. Published numbers do not count the wait for a first appointment, insurance authorization, or clinic scheduling. Ask the clinic for their real start-to-finish time for cancer patients.
What to expect: Not every egg collected will become an embryo, and frozen embryos do not guarantee a pregnancy later. Results vary from person to person. Storage is billed every year, and there are added costs later if you thaw and use the embryos.
Cost, Egg collection cycle: $12,400 on average (US) (Published average is for one egg freezing cycle, which uses the same collection steps. Lab work to create embryos adds costs that are not published.) [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31)]
Cost, Fertility medicines: about $5,000 [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31)]
Cost, Storage: $200 to $500 per year [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31)]

### Sperm banking (established)
You collect semen samples, and the sperm are frozen and stored for possible use in the future.
Who it applies to: anatomy testes; stage not_started/in_treatment.
- Set up with a sperm bank: Your care team refers you to a sperm bank or clinic lab. The bank may require blood tests for infectious diseases before you bank your sperm.
- Before the first collection: It is best not to ejaculate, during sex or masturbation, for 2 to 5 days before the first collection and between collections.
- Collection: At the sperm bank, a staff member brings you to a small private room to collect the sample. Some banks let you collect at home. If you are in the hospital, you can collect the sample in your hospital room.
- More samples if time allows: Banks usually suggest at least 3 samples, scheduled 2 to 3 days apart, and collecting as many as time allows before treatment. If treatment starts soon, you can collect with only 1 day between samples. Even 1 or 2 samples is helpful.
- Freezing and storage: The sperm are frozen and stored. You pay a yearly storage fee for as long as the bank holds your samples.
Timeline: Collection itself is quick. With 3 samples spaced 2 to 3 days apart, banking usually takes about a week. It can be compressed into a few days if treatment starts soon.
What to expect: Doctors advise banking before the first dose of treatment when possible: even a single treatment can affect sperm quality and the DNA inside sperm, and samples collected soon after treatment starts or ends carry a higher risk of genetic damage. If treatment has already started, banking can still be worth discussing with your care team. Any banked sperm can offer a chance at biological parenthood later, and a low sperm count should not stop you from banking. A lab method called ICSI can use a very small amount of sperm. Frozen sperm can be stored indefinitely; sperm frozen for over 20 years have been used to create pregnancies. Storage is billed every year. Banking does not guarantee a pregnancy later.
Cost, Testing, analyzing, and freezing sperm: $500 to $1,000 [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31); American Cancer Society: Preserving Fertility in Children and Teens with Cancer (2025-01-13)]
Cost, Storage: $200 to $500 per year [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31); American Cancer Society: Preserving Fertility in Children and Teens with Cancer (2025-01-13)]
Cost, Example package price: about $800 to $1,000 (One large New York program's price for 3 collections, including the first year of storage. Prices vary by clinic.) [Memorial Sloan Kettering: Sperm Banking (2024-02-22)]
[Sources: ASCO 2025 Fertility Preservation Guideline, Recommendation 3.1 (via COG endorsement) (2025-03-19); Memorial Sloan Kettering: Sperm Banking (2024-02-22); ASCO 2025 Fertility Preservation Guideline, Recommendation 3.5 (via COG endorsement) (2025-03-19); ASRM ReproductiveFacts.org: Cancer and its impact on sperm, cryopreservation, and fertility (2023)]

### Testicular sperm extraction (TESE) (established)
A surgeon collects sperm directly from the testicle for people who cannot provide a semen sample.
Who it applies to: anatomy testes; stage not_started.
- Consult: A fertility specialist reviews your medical history and talks through whether this fits your situation and your treatment schedule.
- Sperm retrieval: A surgeon removes sperm directly from the testicle. This is an option when a semen sample is not possible.
- Freezing and storage: The sperm are frozen and stored. Storage is billed every year.
What to expect: Sperm collected this way can later be injected directly into an egg in a lab process called ICSI, which can work with a very small amount of sperm. Results vary from person to person, and this does not guarantee a pregnancy later. Storage is billed every year.
Cost, Surgical sperm retrieval: not published [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31)]
Cost, Storage: $200 to $500 per year [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31)]
[Sources: ASCO 2025 Fertility Preservation Guideline, Recommendation 3.2 (via COG endorsement) (2025-03-19); ASRM ReproductiveFacts.org: Cancer and its impact on sperm, cryopreservation, and fertility (2023); ASRM ReproductiveFacts.org: Fertility Preservation Journey; UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31); ASCO 2025 Fertility Preservation Guideline, Recommendation 3.5 (via COG endorsement) (2025-03-19)]

### Ovarian tissue freezing (established)
Part or all of an ovary is removed in surgery and the egg-holding tissue is frozen, with no hormone shots needed first.
Who it applies to: anatomy ovaries_uterus; stage not_started.
- Consult: You meet a fertility doctor. Because this method does not need ovarian stimulation, it can be done right away if chemotherapy cannot wait. It also does not require having gone through puberty, so it may be the only option for young children.
- Surgery: An entire ovary, or pieces of an ovary, are removed during a surgery.
- Lab processing and freezing: The tissue goes to a special laboratory. The outer layer of the ovary, which holds the eggs, is removed, cut into small pieces, and frozen.
- Possible future use: Later, doctors can re-implant the tissue into your body, with the hope that eggs in the tissue will mature and be released with ovulation. Another option is to mature eggs from the tissue in the laboratory.
Timeline: No hormone shots are needed first, so this can be scheduled without delaying chemotherapy.
What to expect: Results vary from person to person, and frozen tissue does not guarantee a pregnancy later. Before tissue is placed back, doctors check it carefully because there is a theoretical risk of reintroducing cancer cells, and the importance of that risk is unknown. Re-implanting the tissue means another surgery. Storage is billed every year.
Cost, Surgery, processing, and freezing: not published [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31)]
Cost, Storage: $200 to $500 per year (Published range is for storing eggs or sperm. Tissue storage fees may differ by clinic.) [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31)]
[Sources: ASCO 2025 Fertility Preservation Guideline, Recommendation 4.10 (via COG endorsement) (2025-03-19); Memorial Sloan Kettering: Fertility Preservation Before Cancer Treatment (ovaries and uterus) (2019-10-23)]

### Ovarian suppression (GnRH agonist) (adjunct)
A medicine that quiets the ovaries during chemotherapy. You may hear it offered alongside, not instead of, freezing eggs, embryos, or ovarian tissue. ASCO says this medicine should not be used in place of established methods like egg, embryo, or ovarian tissue freezing. It may be offered as an add-on for people with breast cancer. For other cancers, its benefits and risks still need more study.
Who it applies to: anatomy ovaries_uterus; stage not_started; treatments chemo.
- Medicine during chemotherapy: A GnRH agonist is a medicine that causes the ovaries to shut down and stop making estradiol, a form of estrogen, while you get chemotherapy. If chemotherapy must start urgently, this medicine can still be offered and can help in other ways, such as stopping periods during treatment.
What to expect: This is not a replacement for freezing eggs, embryos, or ovarian tissue. It may be offered as an add-on in breast cancer. One clear use is stopping periods during urgent chemotherapy. It does not guarantee your ovaries will work normally after treatment.
Cost, GnRH agonist medicine: not published [ASCO 2025 Fertility Preservation Guideline, Recommendation 4.8 (via COG endorsement) (2025-03-19)]
[Sources: ASCO 2025 Fertility Preservation Guideline, Recommendation 4.8 (via COG endorsement) (2025-03-19); National Cancer Institute: Female Fertility and Cancer Treatment (2025-05-14); ASCO 2025 Fertility Preservation Guideline, Recommendation 4.9 (via COG endorsement) (2025-03-19)]

### Ovarian transposition (established)
A surgery that moves the ovaries, and sometimes the fallopian tubes, away from the area that will receive radiation.
Who it applies to: anatomy ovaries_uterus; stage not_started; treatments radiation.
- Planning with your team: This is for people who need radiation to the pelvis. Because the ovaries can drift back over time, the surgery is done as close to the start of radiation as possible.
- Surgery: A surgeon moves the ovaries, and sometimes the fallopian tubes, away from the area receiving radiation. It may be done during the same surgery that removes the cancer.
What to expect: Because radiation can scatter, the ovaries are not always protected, and this technique is not always successful. It is not suitable for people with a moderate or high risk of cancer in the ovaries, or for people getting chemotherapy that harms the ovaries at the same time.
Cost, Surgery: not published [ASCO 2025 Fertility Preservation Guideline, Recommendation 4.5 (via COG endorsement) (2025-03-19)]

## Insurance, coverage, and common questions

### Is there a medicine that protects sperm during treatment?
No. Hormone suppression therapy does not preserve fertility in males. It is not effective, and the ASCO guideline says it should not be offered for this purpose. Banking sperm before treatment is the recommended path. [ASCO 2025 Fertility Preservation Guideline, Recommendation 3.3 (via COG endorsement) (2025-03-19); ASCO 2025 Fertility Preservation Guideline, Recommendation 3.1 (via COG endorsement) (2025-03-19)]

### Do the hormone shots feed hormone-sensitive cancers?
This worry comes up a lot with breast and gynecologic cancers. For these cancers, clinics use stimulation protocols built around a medicine called an aromatase inhibitor, which keeps estrogen levels lower during the egg collection cycle. These protocols are well established, and studies have not found an increased risk of the cancer coming back because of them. Your oncologist and fertility doctor plan this together. [ASCO 2025 Fertility Preservation Guideline, Recommendation 4.1 (via COG endorsement) (2025-03-19); ASCO 2025 Fertility Preservation Guideline, Recommendation 4.2 (via COG endorsement) (2025-03-19)]

### If you're LGBTQ+
California's SB 729 defines infertility to include the inability to reproduce either as an individual or with your partner, and bars plans from limiting coverage based on marital status or sexual orientation. In plain terms: not having a partner who produces the other half of an embryo counts, by law. [SB 729 chaptered text (2024), via CalMatters Digital Democracy; RESOLVE: Understanding California's IVF insurance law]
Donor agreements, parentage, and surrogacy are legal questions, and the answers are state-specific. A family-formation attorney is the right person for them; many offer one free consult, and your social worker may know local ones. []

### Your California plan should cover fertility preservation
California recognizes fertility preservation as a covered basic benefit when a medical treatment, like chemotherapy or radiation, could cause infertility. The state's health plan regulator, the Department of Managed Health Care (DMHC), recognizes this under the Knox-Keene Act, and it was spelled out by Senate Bill 600 in 2019. This applies to state-regulated commercial plans, which are most job-based and marketplace plans in California. [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31); SB 600 (2019), via CalMatters Digital Democracy]
When you call your plan, the words matter. Ask about coverage for medically indicated fertility preservation, also called iatrogenic infertility. Say clearly that this is not the same as general infertility benefits. You are asking about preserving fertility before a medical treatment that can harm it. [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31)]
One exception: if your employer's plan is self-funded (the employer pays claims itself), state law does not apply to it. You can ask your plan or HR whether your plan is self-funded or fully insured. [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31)]
If your plan says no, you can appeal, and your doctor can submit the appeal for you. Appeals usually take 30 to 60 days, but urgent cases are decided in 4 days. In California, published Independent Medical Reviews have sided with patients in this situation. [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31)]
- Call member services: Use the number on the back of your insurance card. Say: I have been diagnosed with (your condition), and my planned treatment can harm my future fertility. I am calling to verify coverage for medically indicated fertility preservation, also known as iatrogenic infertility. This is not the same as general infertility benefits.
- Write down the call details: Ask for the agent's name and a reference number, and note the date. You will want these if you need to appeal.
- If denied, ask your doctor to file an urgent appeal: A physician can submit the appeal on your behalf. Urgent appeals are typically decided in 4 days. If the plan still says no, you can contact California's health plan regulator, the DMHC.

### A newer California law may cover fertility treatment, including IVF
California passed a law called SB 729 in September 2024. It applies to plan contracts issued, amended, or renewed on or after January 1, 2026. If your plan renews mid-year, the benefit starts at renewal. [SB 729 chaptered text (2024), via CalMatters Digital Democracy; Sen. Menjivar's office: California State Budget Delays Implementation of SB 729; RESOLVE: Understanding California's IVF insurance law]
If you are in a large-group, fully insured plan, it must cover the diagnosis and treatment of infertility and fertility services, including IVF: up to 3 completed egg retrievals with unlimited embryo transfers. Small-group plans only have to offer this coverage, and your employer decides whether to buy it. Self-funded employer plans and Medi-Cal are not included, and religious employers are exempt. CalPERS plans start July 1, 2027. [SB 729 chaptered text (2024), via CalMatters Digital Democracy; RESOLVE: Understanding California's IVF insurance law]
The law's definition of infertility is broad. It includes a doctor's findings based on your medical history and testing, and it includes not being able to have a child, on your own or with your partner, without medical help. You do not have to be married or have a partner, and there is no required months-of-trying clock when a doctor's findings establish it. [SB 729 chaptered text (2024), via CalMatters Digital Democracy]
- Ask HR two questions: Is our plan fully insured or self-funded? And is it a large-group plan? The SB 729 requirement applies to large-group, fully insured plans.
- Ask when your plan year renews: The benefit applies to plans issued, amended, or renewed on or after January 1, 2026. If your plan has not renewed yet, the benefit may not have started.

### Finding out where your fertility stands now
If your treatment could affect the ovaries, a yearly check-up is recommended. It covers your periods, pregnancy history, and sexual health, and blood may be tested for hormone levels (FSH, LH, and estradiol) if a problem is suspected. Regular monthly periods with normal hormone levels are a good sign for fertility. [Children's Oncology Group Health Link: Ovarian and Reproductive Health after Cancer Treatment, v6.0 (2023-10)]
A fertility evaluation can tell you more. It usually includes blood tests for hormones related to fertility, including FSH and AMH, and a transvaginal ultrasound to count growing follicles in the ovaries (the antral follicle count). [Memorial Sloan Kettering: Fertility Evaluation and Preservation After Cancer Treatment (ovaries and uterus) (2019-10-23)]
Timing matters, and waiting is normal. Memorial Sloan Kettering generally recommends waiting at least 1 year after completing cancer treatment before having your fertility evaluated. Also, because of how eggs develop, there may be no egg yield within 3 months of the last chemotherapy dose, and doctors counsel that eggs collected close to treatment carry unknowns. [Memorial Sloan Kettering: Fertility Evaluation and Preservation After Cancer Treatment (ovaries and uterus) (2019-10-23); ASCO 2025 Fertility Preservation Guideline, Recommendation 4.3 (via COG endorsement) (2025-03-19)]
If you did not freeze eggs or embryos before treatment, that door is not closed. ASCO says egg or embryo freezing may still be offered after treatment to people at risk of early ovarian insufficiency or infertility. [ASCO 2025 Fertility Preservation Guideline, Recommendation 4.3 (via COG endorsement) (2025-03-19)]
- Ask for a referral to a fertility specialist: ASCO recommends ongoing fertility discussions and referrals in the years after treatment, including when you are thinking about pregnancy. You do not have to be sure about wanting children to ask.

### Finding out where your fertility stands now
If your treatment could affect the testes, a yearly check-up is recommended. Blood may be tested for hormone levels: a morning (AM) testosterone, LH, FSH, and inhibin. [Children's Oncology Group Health Link: Testicular and Reproductive Health after Cancer Treatment, v6.0 (2023-10)]
The best way to assess the ability to make sperm is a semen analysis. It measures how many sperm there are, how they move, and how they look. The sample is given after several days without ejaculating. If giving a sample is not possible, or you would rather not, FSH and inhibin blood levels can give some insight. [Children's Oncology Group Health Link: Testicular and Reproductive Health after Cancer Treatment, v6.0 (2023-10)]
Timing matters, and waiting is normal. Memorial Sloan Kettering says you can have a semen analysis done at least 1 year after your cancer treatment is complete, and that full recovery can take up to 5 years. When sperm production recovers, it usually happens in the first few years after treatment ends. [Memorial Sloan Kettering: Building Your Family After Cancer Treatment (people born with testicles) (2023-06-21); Children's Oncology Group Health Link: Testicular and Reproductive Health after Cancer Treatment, v6.0 (2023-10)]
One result does not settle it. No sperm on a single sample is not a final answer; it takes more than one sample to point to infertility. A low sperm count does not rule out having children, since fertility specialists have ways to work with small numbers of sperm. [Children's Oncology Group Health Link: Testicular and Reproductive Health after Cancer Treatment, v6.0 (2023-10)]
- Ask for a referral to a fertility specialist: ASCO recommends ongoing fertility discussions and referrals in the years after treatment. If you banked sperm before treatment, a fertility specialist can review your options for using it.

### What giving a sample is actually like
Here is the plain version. At the sperm bank, a staff member brings you to a small private room, you masturbate, and you collect the semen in a sterile cup. That is the whole procedure. Some banks let you collect at home instead. If you are in the hospital, you can collect the sample in your hospital room. [Memorial Sloan Kettering: Sperm Banking (2024-02-22)]
To prepare: do not ejaculate, during sex or masturbation, for 2 to 5 days before the first collection and between collections. Do not use saliva or lubricants, because these can destroy sperm. You can ask for mineral oil if it helps, but use it only at the base of the penis. [Memorial Sloan Kettering: Sperm Banking (2024-02-22)]
If there is time, banks usually suggest 3 samples with 2 to 3 days between them. If treatment starts soon, you can collect with only 1 day between samples. Even 1 or 2 samples is helpful. The bank may also require blood tests for infectious diseases before banking. [Memorial Sloan Kettering: Sperm Banking (2024-02-22)]
Feeling awkward about this is common, and it is nothing to apologize for. Many teens are not comfortable masturbating to produce a sample, especially if parents are part of the conversation. You can ask for privacy, and if collecting this way is not possible for any reason, tell your doctor or nurse. There are other ways to collect sperm, including collection condoms used during sex and procedures that take sperm directly from the testicle. [American Cancer Society: Preserving Fertility in Children and Teens with Cancer (2025-01-13); Memorial Sloan Kettering: Sperm Banking (2024-02-22); ASRM ReproductiveFacts.org: Cancer and its impact on sperm, cryopreservation, and fertility (2023)]

### Check your partner's plan too
Fertility benefits come from the plan, not from who carries the card. People are often covered as a spouse or dependent on someone else's employer plan, and benefit checks work the same way; you just also need the policyholder's name and date of birth when you call. [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31)]
If you and your partner each have coverage, it is worth checking both plans with the same questions: is the plan fully insured or self-funded, what state is it based in, and are fertility preservation codes billable? Plans differ, and one may cover what the other does not. [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31)]

## Financial aid programs
- Men Having Babies: Discounted or free fertility and surrogacy services, plus grants, for gay prospective parents. The program runs in two stages. Eligibility: Gay men and transgender women becoming first-time parents. Amount: not published. [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31)]
- Allyson Whitney Foundation: Life Interrupted Grants of $500 to $1,500 for young adults with rare cancers. Grants can go toward fertility costs like egg retrieval and sperm banking, as well as rent and medical bills. Eligibility: US citizen, age 16 to 36, with a rare cancer diagnosis, in active treatment or within 10 months after it. You cannot apply on your own: a facility, such as an oncology social worker, doctor's office, or patient navigator, must request the application for you, and each facility can receive only one award per grant year. Applications open once a year in a spring window, with decisions by about August 1. Awards depend on available funds. Amount: $500 to $1,500 per grant. [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31); Allyson Whitney Foundation: Life Interrupted Grants]
- Chick Mission: Hope Grants that cover the costs of one egg freezing cycle before cancer treatment. Medication costs are not included. The grant is paid directly to a fertility clinic in their network, so you do not pay first and wait for repayment. Eligibility: Female, age 18 to 40, with a cancer diagnosis and a treatment plan that an oncologist says puts fertility at risk, before any chemo or radiation. Available in California, Colorado, Florida, Illinois, Montana, New Jersey, New York, Oklahoma, Pennsylvania, and Texas. Income limits apply (under $150,000 single or $200,000 married). A fertility clinic in their network submits the application for you, before your cycle starts. Amount: not published. [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31); The Chick Mission: Hope Grants]
- Expect Miracles Foundation SAMFund: Grants for young adults with cancer who have finished active treatment. Family Building grants can help pay for fertility treatment and other paths to parenthood after cancer. Grant amounts vary, and not every applicant can be funded. Eligibility: Age 21 to 39 at the time of application, with active cancer treatment completed. Applications open twice a year for about 3 weeks each (Family Building grants usually open in March and September). You hear back 6 to 7 weeks after a cycle closes. As of November 2026 the fall cycles are closed, and the next ones open in early 2027. Amount: not published. [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31); Expect Miracles Foundation: Get Help (SAMFund grants)]
- Fertility Within Reach: Education and advocacy for getting fertility treatment and preservation covered, plus the Banking on the Future grant, which has helped young cancer patients pay for the first year of storage. Eligibility: Their website was not reachable when we checked in November 2026, so current program details could not be confirmed. An oncology social worker can help you check whether the grant is still active before you apply. Amount: not published. [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31); Fertility Within Reach homepage (Internet Archive snapshot of 2026-10-14; live site returned 404 on 2026-11-02)]
- Livestrong Fertility: Discounted fertility preservation through partner clinics (at least 25 percent off services) plus free fertility medication from a pharmaceutical partner for approved patients. Discounts are applied at the clinic, so you do not pay full price first and wait for repayment. Eligibility: Lawfully present in the US with a confirmed cancer diagnosis, before treatment. Your oncology team confirms that treatment poses a risk to fertility, you choose a Livestrong partner fertility clinic, and you show that insurance will not cover preservation. Egg and embryo freezing applicants must be uninsured or have limited coverage; sperm banking applicants also have income limits (household income of $101,000 or less if single, $130,000 or less if married). Amount: at least 25 percent off partner clinic services, plus free medication for approved patients. [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31); Livestrong Fertility program page]
- NMDP (formerly Be The Match): Fertility preservation grants of up to $10,000 for people with sickle cell disease before a transplant. Covers egg retrieval and freezing, semen analysis and freezing, storage fees, and medication. Eligibility: Diagnosis of sickle cell disease, preparing for a transplant, with household income at or below about 350 percent of the federal poverty level. The application must go in before preservation begins, and a transplant center social worker or NMDP patient navigator applies on your behalf. Amount: up to $10,000. [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31); NMDP: Financial assistance before and after transplant]
- ReUnite Oncofertility: Discounted fertility medication for cancer patients preserving fertility before treatment. The discount can cover up to 100 percent of what you would pay for covered medicines, based on income and clinical need, and is applied when the pharmacy fills the order. Eligibility: Female patients whose cancer treatment can affect fertility, with no prior chemotherapy, who are paying for the medication themselves without fertility drug coverage. Covered medicines are Follistim AQ cartridge, ganirelix acetate injection, and Pregnyl, with per-cycle limits. You apply online with proof of income, and your oncologist completes a form. Amount: up to 100 percent off covered medications. [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31); ReUnite Rx: Discount Programs (Oncofertility)]
- Team Maggie's Dream: Fertility assistance grants of up to $2,500 for teens and young adults with cancer, for preservation costs like egg retrieval and sperm banking. Funds are paid directly to the medical provider, not to you. Eligibility: US citizen, age 15 to 37, receiving cancer treatment, uninsured or denied fertility preservation coverage by your insurance carrier. You mail in an application with proof of financial need and medical treatment, plus a short paragraph about why you are applying. Award size depends on available funds. Amount: up to $2,500 per grant. [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31); Team Maggie's Dream: Grants]
- Verna's Purse (ReproTech): Reduced rates on long-term storage of eggs, sperm, embryos, ovarian tissue, or testicular tissue through ReproTech, for people with cancer-related fertility needs who would have trouble paying storage fees. Eligibility: People storing specimens with ReproTech whose fertility is affected by cancer. There are separate applications for egg, sperm, and tissue storage and for embryo storage, and the discount is set case by case. Blood testing for infectious disease is required. Amount: not published. [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31); ReproTech: Storage Discount Programs (Verna's Purse)]
- Walgreens Specialty Pharmacy fertility preservation program: Free fertility medication, donated by Ferring Pharmaceuticals through its Heart Beat program, for women preserving fertility before cancer treatment. Covers up to 2 preservation cycles, and the pharmacy supplies the medication at no cost. Eligibility: Female, age 43 or younger (over age 40, an AMH blood test result above 0.5 is needed), diagnosed with cancer within the last 12 months, before any treatment that could harm fertility. There are no income requirements. Not available in Massachusetts, New Jersey, or Arkansas. Your care team contacts the pharmacy to enroll you. Amount: free medication for up to 2 cycles. [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31); Walgreens Specialty Pharmacy: Fertility Preservation program]
- Worth the Wait: Grants of $500 to $2,500 for fertility preservation before cancer treatment (Emergency Preservation Grants) and for family building after treatment, including frozen embryo transfer, egg, embryo, or semen preservation, storage fees, adoption, and surrogacy. Grants are paid directly to the clinic or agency. Eligibility: Age 13 to 39 with a cancer diagnosis, not yet a parent, receiving care at a US facility or working with a US agency, with financial hardship shown on an IRS tax form and no fertility insurance coverage. Emergency grants must be used within 60 days and family building grants within 120 days. Amount: $500 to $2,500 per grant. [UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31); Worth the Wait Charity: Request Help]

## Everyday health facts
- Sperm take about 2 and a half months to develop. A sperm cell takes over 70 days to grow and mature. So anything that harms sperm, or stops harming them, takes about 3 months to show up in a sample. [ASRM ReproductiveFacts.org: Optimizing Male Fertility (2023)]
- Heat lowers sperm production. The testicles hang outside the body to stay a few degrees cooler than the rest of you, because sperm develop best at 97 to 99 degrees. Hot tubs, saunas, and a laptop resting on the lap all add heat. [Cleveland Clinic: Male Reproductive System (2023-05-08); ASRM ReproductiveFacts.org: Optimizing Male Fertility (2023)]
- Taking testosterone or anabolic steroids shuts down sperm production. Outside testosterone switches off the brain signals that tell the testicles to make sperm, often driving counts very low or to zero. Production usually returns over about 3 to 12 months after stopping. [ASRM ReproductiveFacts.org: Testosterone Use and Male Infertility (2021); Cleveland Clinic: Can Drugs Lower Your Sperm Count? (2022-11-10)]
- Cannabis can lower sperm count. THC can interfere with testosterone production, which sperm making depends on. Caveat: Studies in the general population link marijuana use to lower semen quality; studies in fertility clinics are mixed. [Cleveland Clinic: Can Drugs Lower Your Sperm Count? (2022-11-10); ASRM Practice Committee: Tobacco or Marijuana Use and Infertility (2024)]
- Smoking speeds up the loss of eggs, and lost eggs do not come back. Chemicals in cigarette smoke, including nicotine, cyanide, and carbon monoxide, speed up the rate eggs are lost. Menopause comes 1 to 4 years earlier in people who smoke. [ASRM ReproductiveFacts.org: Smoking and Infertility (2023)]
- Smoking lowers sperm count and movement. Smoke chemicals can damage sperm DNA and increase the share of abnormally shaped sperm. [ASRM ReproductiveFacts.org: Smoking and Infertility (2023); ASRM Practice Committee: Tobacco or Marijuana Use and Infertility (2024)]
- Egg supply is set at birth; age and egg count are not things anyone can change. A person is born with all the eggs they will ever have. No treatment grows new ones. Of the everyday factors, smoking is the clearly proven harm to avoid. [ASRM ReproductiveFacts.org: Does My Age Affect My Fertility? (2023); ASRM ReproductiveFacts.org: Smoking and Infertility (2023)]
- Long-term opioid use can lower sperm quantity and quality. Opioids disrupt the brain signals that control testosterone. [Cleveland Clinic: Can Drugs Lower Your Sperm Count? (2022-11-10)]
- Heavy drinking is linked to reduced fertility. ASRM defines heavy as more than 2 drinks a day. In men, heavy alcohol use can lower testosterone, which sperm production depends on. [ASRM ReproductiveFacts.org: Optimizing Natural Fertility (2023); Cleveland Clinic: Can Drugs Lower Your Sperm Count? (2022-11-10)]
- Some popular advice has no evidence behind it. There is no proof that fertility supplements work, and position or lying down after sex does not change the odds of conception. Skipping those saves money and worry. [ASRM ReproductiveFacts.org: Optimizing Natural Fertility (2023)]

## Comparing options (worded grades, no rankings)
Considerations: Time before treatment; What it involves; Upfront cost (published); Ongoing cost; Evidence status; Decisions needed now.
- egg_freezing: Takes a couple of weeks and real money, keeps every later choice open. time_needed: About 2 to 3 weeks once started; what_it_involves: Daily shots, then retrieval while asleep; upfront_cost: Higher: $12,400 plus about $5,000 medicines; ongoing_cost: $200 to $500 per year storage; evidence: Established; decisions_now: No sperm decision needed. [ASCO 2025 Fertility Preservation Guideline, Recommendation 4.2 (via COG endorsement) (2025-03-19); UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31)]
- embryo_freezing: Same path as egg freezing, plus deciding whose sperm, now. time_needed: About 2 to 3 weeks once started; what_it_involves: Daily shots, retrieval, lab fertilization; upfront_cost: Higher: $12,400 plus about $5,000 medicines; ongoing_cost: $200 to $500 per year storage; evidence: Established; decisions_now: Sperm source decided now. [ASCO 2025 Fertility Preservation Guideline, Recommendation 4.1 (via COG endorsement) (2025-03-19); UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31)]
- sperm_banking: The fastest, simplest, cheapest option on this list. time_needed: Days, compressible to 1 or 2; what_it_involves: Private collection, no procedure; upfront_cost: Lower: $500 to $1,000; ongoing_cost: $200 to $500 per year storage; evidence: Established; decisions_now: None beyond doing it. [ASCO 2025 Fertility Preservation Guideline, Recommendation 3.1 (via COG endorsement) (2025-03-19); UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31)]
- testicular_sperm_extraction: The surgical route when a sample isn't possible. time_needed: One procedure visit; what_it_involves: A surgeon retrieves sperm directly; upfront_cost: Not published; ongoing_cost: $200 to $500 per year storage; evidence: Established; decisions_now: None beyond doing it. [ASCO 2025 Fertility Preservation Guideline, Recommendation 3.2 (via COG endorsement) (2025-03-19); UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31)]
- ovarian_tissue_freezing: Fast because it skips stimulation; the trade is surgery. time_needed: No hormone-shot wait; what_it_involves: Surgery under anesthesia; upfront_cost: Not published; ongoing_cost: $200 to $500 per year storage; evidence: Established; decisions_now: No sperm decision needed. [ASCO 2025 Fertility Preservation Guideline, Recommendation 4.10 (via COG endorsement) (2025-03-19); UC San Diego Moores Cancer Center & Alliance for Fertility Preservation toolkit (2025-07-31)]
- ovarian_suppression: Easy to add, but guidelines say it never replaces freezing. time_needed: Given during chemo; what_it_involves: An injection; upfront_cost: Not published; ongoing_cost: No storage; evidence: An add-on, not a replacement; decisions_now: None. [ASCO 2025 Fertility Preservation Guideline, Recommendation 4.8 (via COG endorsement) (2025-03-19)]
- ovarian_transposition: For pelvic radiation specifically: shields rather than stores. time_needed: Scheduled before radiation; what_it_involves: Surgery to move the ovaries; upfront_cost: Not published; ongoing_cost: No storage; evidence: Established for pelvic radiation; decisions_now: None beyond scheduling. [ASCO 2025 Fertility Preservation Guideline, Recommendation 4.5 (via COG endorsement) (2025-03-19)]

## Thinking it through (the reflection path, for people unsure about children)
A few short screens to think it through. No right answers, nothing saved, and unsure is a real place to land.
- This is two decisions, not one: Whether to preserve fertility now is a decision with a clock, set by your treatment schedule. Whether to become a parent someday has no clock. You can answer the first without answering the second. [Ottawa Personal Decision Guide (Ottawa Hospital Research Institute) (2025); BEFORE fertility decision aid (Speller et al., BMC Med Inform Decis Mak 2019) (2019)]
- Where are you, honestly?: Tap the one that fits today. Every square on this board is a fine place to stand. [Ottawa Personal Decision Guide (Ottawa Hospital Research Institute) (2025); BEFORE fertility decision aid (Speller et al., BMC Med Inform Decis Mak 2019) (2019)]
- What could being a parent mean to you?: People mean different things by it. Sitting with these for a minute is the work. [Cancer, Fertility and Me (Jones et al., Frontiers in Oncology 2022) (2022); CDC Reproductive Life Plan]
- Plans are allowed to change: Public health guidance on reproductive life planning is built on one observation: people's plans change. What you feel during cancer treatment does not have to be your final answer, in either direction. [CDC Reproductive Life Plan]
- What preserving does, and doesn't, promise: Preserving keeps a possibility open. It does not guarantee a child later, and storage costs continue each year. Deciding with that honest picture beats deciding with a hopeful blur. [BEFORE fertility decision aid (Speller et al., BMC Med Inform Decis Mak 2019) (2019); Cancer, Fertility and Me (Jones et al., Frontiers in Oncology 2022) (2022)]
- There is more than one road to parenthood: Donor eggs or sperm, surrogacy, adoption, fostering. Preserving your own eggs or sperm is one road, not the only one. Some people find this the single most relieving fact on these screens. [BEFORE fertility decision aid (Speller et al., BMC Med Inform Decis Mak 2019) (2019); Cancer, Fertility and Me (Jones et al., Frontiers in Oncology 2022) (2022)]
- What matters most to you?: Slide each one. There is no score and nothing is saved; the point is noticing which sliders you push hard. [Ottawa Personal Decision Guide (Ottawa Hospital Research Institute) (2025); Cancer, Fertility and Me (Jones et al., Frontiers in Oncology 2022) (2022); BEFORE fertility decision aid (Speller et al., BMC Med Inform Decis Mak 2019) (2019)]
- Who to talk with, and the pressure check: A partner, a parent, a close friend, a counselor, your care team. One more question worth asking yourself, from the Ottawa guide: is anyone pressuring you? Other people can have expectations; the decision is still yours alone, and no one else's expectations are a reason. Deciding later is itself a decision you are allowed to make: waiting keeps after-treatment options and aid programs open, while some pre-treatment options need time before treatment starts. Your care team can tell you how much time you actually have. [Ottawa Personal Decision Guide (Ottawa Hospital Research Institute) (2025); BEFORE fertility decision aid (Speller et al., BMC Med Inform Decis Mak 2019) (2019)]

## Family building after treatment (Paths to Parenthood)
There is more than one way to a family after cancer, and none of them is the consolation prize. Start with the fertility check under Health, then read the paths that fit. Your care team and a fertility specialist help you pick.

### Using what you froze
Frozen eggs, sperm, or embryos go back to work through lab treatments.
Frozen embryos are thawed and placed in the uterus at the right point in a cycle. Frozen eggs are combined with sperm in the lab (IVF), sometimes by injecting a single sperm into an egg (ICSI). Banked sperm can be used for IVF or placed in a partner's uterus with a thin tube (IUI). [American Cancer Society: Fertility Assistance After Cancer (2025-01-17)]
Thawing and treatment are billed separately from the yearly storage you have been paying. Prices vary widely and are rarely published, so ask the clinic for an itemized list before you start. [American Cancer Society: Fertility Assistance After Cancer (2025-01-17); Memorial Sloan Kettering: Building Your Family After Cancer Treatment (people born with testicles) (2023-06-21)]

### Trying for pregnancy
Often possible after treatment. The timeline is set with your oncologist.
Start with the fertility check. On timing, advice differs: some providers suggest waiting at least about 6 months after chemotherapy, others about 2 to 5 years, depending on your cancer, treatment, and age. Your oncologist sets the timeline that fits your situation. [American Cancer Society: Having a Baby After Cancer, Pregnancy (2024-05-28)]
In general, pregnancy has not been shown to make cancer more likely to come back. For hormone-positive breast cancer there is direct evidence: in a trial of 516 young women who paused their endocrine therapy to try for pregnancy, about 74% became pregnant, and after 3 years their cancer outcomes matched similar women who had not paused. [American Cancer Society: Having a Baby After Cancer, Pregnancy (2024-05-28); POSITIVE trial (Partridge et al., New England Journal of Medicine 2023) (2023-05-04)]
If you had radiation to the pelvis, the uterus can be affected in ways that raise the chance of miscarriage, early birth, and low birth weight. That does not close the door, but it belongs in the conversation with your care team before trying. [American Cancer Society: Having a Baby After Cancer, Pregnancy (2024-05-28)]

### Donor eggs, sperm, or embryos
A screened donor supplies what treatment took away.
Donors are screened for infections and genetic conditions. Donor eggs are fertilized in the lab and the embryo is placed in your uterus, so you can carry and give birth even when your own eggs cannot be used. Donor sperm is quarantined for about 180 days, retested, and usually used with IUI. Donor embryos come from couples who finished IVF with embryos to spare. [Alliance for Fertility Preservation: Post-Treatment Family Building (2026-11-17); American Cancer Society: Fertility Assistance After Cancer (2025-01-17)]
Counseling is recommended before using donors, including how you might one day explain it to a child, and a lawyer should review your state's parentage rules. [Alliance for Fertility Preservation: Post-Treatment Family Building (2026-11-17)]

### A gestational carrier
Someone else carries the pregnancy, often using your embryo.
A gestational carrier carries an embryo made from your eggs or sperm (or donors'), so the child can be genetically yours even when pregnancy is not safe or possible for you, including after radiation that affected the uterus. Carriers go through medical and psychological evaluation. [Alliance for Fertility Preservation: Post-Treatment Family Building (2026-11-17); American Cancer Society: Fertility Assistance After Cancer (2025-01-17)]
It is one of the most expensive paths, and the law differs state by state, so a lawyer is essential, not optional. Agencies can match you with a carrier, or some people ask a friend or family member. [Alliance for Fertility Preservation: Post-Treatment Family Building (2026-11-17); American Cancer Society: Fertility Assistance After Cancer (2025-01-17)]

### Adoption and fostering
A full path to parenthood, with some cancer-specific paperwork.
Some adoption agencies have real experience with cancer survivors. Expect health questions in the process: agencies commonly ask for medical documentation and a letter from your doctor about your health status. The Americans with Disabilities Act protects survivors against discrimination in adoption. [American Cancer Society: Fertility Assistance After Cancer (2025-01-17)]
Published cost ranges run from about $6,000 through public foster-care adoption to about $20,000 to $50,000 for private or international adoption. Fostering is the lowest-cost route and has its own path to adopting. [American Cancer Society: Fertility Assistance After Cancer (2025-01-17)]

### A full life without children
A real path, chosen by many people with and without a cancer history.
Plenty of people decide not to parent, cancer or no cancer. If you and a partner land in different places on this, counseling has a track record of helping couples work through it. The journey tracker here treats this outcome exactly like the others, because it is one. [American Cancer Society: Fertility Assistance After Cancer (2025-01-17)]

## Questions worth bringing to the care team
- (My risk and timing) How could my treatment plan affect my ability to have children later?
- (My risk and timing) Could my treatment plan be adjusted or timed to allow fertility preservation first?
- (My options) I'm not sure yet what I want. Can you still refer me to a fertility specialist?
- (My options) Which preservation options fit my body, my diagnosis, and my treatment plan?
- (My risk and timing) How much time does each option take, and how soon would I need to start?
- (My options) If my chemotherapy cannot wait, is ovarian tissue freezing an option for me?
- (My options) Is hormone stimulation safe with my type of cancer?
- (My options) I will have radiation near my pelvis. Would surgery to move my ovaries out of the radiation field make sense for me?
- (My options) What if I can't give a semen sample? Are there other ways to collect sperm?
- (Where I stand now) Which tests would tell us about my fertility now?
- (Where I stand now) How long should I wait after treatment before having my fertility checked?
- (Where I stand now) Is freezing eggs or embryos still possible for me now that treatment is over?
- (Insurance and cost) Can your office give me my diagnosis code (ICD-10) and the list of procedure codes (CPT) so I can check my insurance coverage?
- (Insurance and cost) If my insurance says no, would you submit an appeal saying fertility preservation is medically necessary for me?
- (Insurance and cost) Who on the team can help me with costs and insurance, like a social worker or financial counselor?
- (Support) Can I talk this through with a counselor before deciding?
